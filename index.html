M26 Rear Load Cases

This page answers one question: when the car lands, corners, brakes or hits something, how hard is each rear suspension member pushed or pulled, and will it survive? It answers it the same way you would by hand, one step at a time, and shows every equation with the real numbers in it.

How it works, in five moves

  1. Load on one tire. From the car + driver weight and CG height, find the static load on one rear tire, and how much extra load shifts onto it per g of braking, accelerating or cornering.
  2. Tire force for each scenario. Each load case (max bump, cornering, braking…) is a set of g's. Multiply them out to get a 3D force vector at the tire contact patch.
  3. Hardpoints. Take the CAD coordinates for the position the suspension is actually in for that case (bump, droop or ride height). Turn them into the direction each member points.
  4. Equilibrium solve. The trailing arm and upright act as one rigid body. It's held by the TA pivot, two camber links and the shock. Because it isn't moving, the forces and the moments both sum to zero. That's 6 equations for 6 unknown forces, solved as a 6×6 matrix.
  5. Check the parts. A link being pulled is checked against yield, and a link being pushed against buckling. The peak force at every joint is collected for lug and bolt sizing.

Assumptions behind the whole page

  • Quasi-static. Each case is a frozen instant at peak load. The g's stand in for the dynamics.
  • Two-force members. The camber links and the shock have rod ends or spherical bearings at both ends, so they can only push or pull along their own axis. No bending.
  • TA pivot is a ball joint. It carries force but no moment. That's what makes the problem solvable by hand (statically determinate).
  • Rigid parts, one corner at a time, with the tire force acting at the centre of the contact patch.

How to read this page

  • Boxes with a blue border are inputs. Change any of them and everything below updates.
  • Grey panels show the equations worked out for the case picked in the left rail (“Worked example case”).
  • Sign convention: links T = tension (being pulled, +), C = compression (being pushed, −).
  • Margin pills: +0.25+ comfortable, +0.10 tight, −0.20 fails.

Results at a glance

01

Load on one rear tire

What this step does

Works out three numbers that every load case is built from: the static load on one rear tire, and how much load shifts onto that tire for every 1 g of longitudinal or lateral acceleration.

Why weight shifts

The car's mass sits at the CG, inches above the ground, but the tires push at ground level. When the car accelerates, that offset creates a moment, and the only way to balance it is for one axle (or side) to carry more load and the other less. A higher CG or a shorter wheelbase/track means more transfer.

Inputs

Rear roll-stiffness share = the fraction of total lateral load transfer the rear axle takes. It depends on springs and anti-roll bars. 0.521 is a placeholder equal to the weight bias.

02

Load cases → tire contact-patch force

What this step does

Each load case describes one scenario as a few g multipliers. Those multipliers turn the step 01 numbers into a 3D force vector that the ground pushes on the tire with.

The model

The vertical load comes first: the base load times the vertical g, plus weight transfer if the case has it. The tire's sideways and fore-aft grip forces are then that vertical load times the lateral and longitudinal g's.

03

Hardpoints & member directions

What this step does

Takes the CAD coordinates of every joint and turns them into two things the solver needs:

  • Unit vectors : the direction each member points. A two-force member can only push or pull along this line, so its whole force is one number times .
  • Lever arms : where each force acts, measured from the TA pivot. These give the moments.

Which position?

Each case uses the position the suspension would really be in: max bump for landings, full droop for braking (the rear unloads), static ride height for the rest. Each set can have its own CAD origin, because only differences between points are ever used.

04

Equilibrium solve: 6 equations, 6 unknowns

This is the heart of the page. Everything before it sets up the numbers. Everything after it checks the parts using the forces found here.

4.1

Draw the free body

Cut the trailing arm + upright out as one rigid piece (dashed outline in the schematic up top). Five things act on it:

the tire force from step 02, known
the TA pivot reaction. A ball joint can push in any direction, so it has 3 unknowns
the upper camber link: 1 unknown along its known direction
the lower camber link: 1 unknown
the shock: 1 unknown

That's 6 unknowns. In 3D a rigid body has exactly 6 equilibrium equations, so the problem is solvable.

4.2

Forces must add to zero (3 equations)

The part isn't accelerating, so every force on it sums to zero. This is one vector equation, which is three scalar equations (x, y, z).

4.3

Moments about the pivot must add to zero (3 more)

It isn't rotating either, so the moments sum to zero. We take moments about the TA pivot on purpose: acts right at the pivot, so its lever arm is zero and it drops out. The 3 moment equations then only involve .

05

Upright → trailing arm joint

What this step does

Now cut the upright away from the trailing arm. The upright has three loads on it: the tire force and the two camber-link forces. Whatever doesn't cancel out has to be carried by the bolted joint at TA OUT, as a force and a moment.

Why you want it

This is the load case for the TA–upright bolts and for the outboard end of the trailing arm. Use these vectors as the load in FEA of the trailing arm.

The sum runs over the three upright loads, each crossed with its own lever arm from TA OUT. The old sheet crossed the link lever arms with the tire force by mistake.

06

Camber link checks: yield and buckling

Pulled (tension)

A link in tension fails by yielding, so the allowable stress is just the material's yield strength .

Pushed (compression)

A long, thin link in compression bows sideways and buckles well before it yields. How badly depends on its slenderness . Short columns use the Johnson formula, long ones use Euler. The crossover is , and the page picks the right formula for you.

Column curve: where each case sits

The blue line is the allowable compressive stress for this link at any slenderness: Johnson up to the transition, Euler after it. All cases share the same link, so they sit on one vertical line at its slenderness. Each dot is (applied stress × design factor) for a case where the link is in compression. A dot above the blue line fails. To pass, raise the curve (stiffer or stronger material) or move left (bigger r, shorter L).

What to look for. The upper link is in compression in the landing cases, and that's where buckling governs. Its slenderness (~78) is past the aluminum transition (~70), so it's in the Euler region, where only E and r help. A stronger alloy of aluminum does nothing there.

07

Design loads for joints

What this step does

Scans every case ticked “Use for joints” in step 02 and pulls out the peak load at each joint, along with which case caused it. These are the numbers to size lugs, bolts, rod ends and brackets with. Lug and bolt checks will live on their own page.

Why tension and compression are separate

A lug is checked differently depending on direction. Tension drives tear-out and net-section failure, while compression mostly loads the bearing face. So both peaks are listed for each two-force member.

08

What was fixed from Rear_Load_Cases_4.xlsx

The 6×6 solve itself was right: with the original inputs, this page reproduces the old sheet's link, shock and pivot forces to within 0.000001 lbf. The problems were in the bookkeeping around it.

  • 1UCL one-wheel row used steel properties. Results!Q17/S17 pointed at an empty F12, so the 6061 tube got 4130 yield and modulus. With aluminum it actually fails in buckling (step 06).
  • 2Upright moment used the wrong force. The UCL/LCL moment rows crossed their lever arms with the tire force instead of the link forces. Fixed in step 05.
  • 3Trailing arm pivot “stress” was garbage. It divided by the UCL's area or by empty cells. The pivot is now reported as a force (steps 04 and 07).
  • 4Inconsistent link inputs. One LCL row was 6061 while the rest were 4130, and one UCL row had a 0.63 ID instead of 0.62. Each link now has one material and one section.
  • 5Johnson used past its range. The aluminum links sit above the Johnson/Euler transition, so Euler applies. The formula is now picked automatically.
  • 6Bolts & lugs moved out. The old Bolts&Lugs tab had a hard-coded shock force, a degrees/radians bug in shear tear-out ( should be ), and 16 TPI used for the 5/16"-18 preload. Fix those on the lug page, fed by step 07.

Still assumptions, not bugs: the rear roll-stiffness share equals the weight bias (0.521) until you have real roll stiffness numbers. μ = 0.85 from the M25 test isn't used, because grip is set by each case's g's. Tension and compression yield are taken as equal, which is fine for these alloys.
